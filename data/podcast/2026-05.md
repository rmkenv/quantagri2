**THE HOOK**

The May 2 issue opened with a stark warning: cotton was sitting at a 52‑week peak while its spectral velocity turned sharply negative. In that first week of the month, the satellite‑derived NDVI for Xinjiang cotton was –0.11433/16 days, a z‑score of –1.4, yet the front‑month cotton contract was trading at a full 100 % of its 52‑week high. That divergence—price at the roof, signal in decline—was the headline, and it set the tone for a month that would see price and vegetation increasingly out of sync.

**SIGNAL SCORECARD**

Which calls held up, which faltered, and which stayed open?

*Soybeans.* The May 2 call that Iowa soybeans were greening (+0.239/16 days) was confirmed by the price lift to $1,209.00 on May 8. The subsequent drop to $1,194.25 on May 9 and back up to $1,197.25 on May 23 show a volatile but ultimately upward trajectory. The Mato Grosso node’s negative velocity of –0.16108/16 days on May 8 was not reflected in price, which remained near‑peak, a clear divergence that persisted through the month.

*Corn.* The May 2 warning that Iowa corn was in decline (–0.0733/16 days) matched the price fall from $468.25 to $456.25 on May 9, and further to $455.25 on May 16. The rebound to $463.75 on May 23 was only partial, suggesting the negative velocity had not fully translated into a price correction.

*Wheat.* The May 2 positive velocity (+0.22132/16 days) was not mirrored by a price rise; wheat fell from $624.50 to $607.50 on May 9, then recovered to $635.50 on May 16 and $647.00 on May 23. The velocity had plateaued by May 9, and the price bounce suggests a lagged response.

*Sugar.* The May 2 signal of a bearish, positive velocity (+0.042/16 days) was not priced into a decline; sugar traded at 82 % of its high on May 2. By May 16 it had risen to 85 % of its high, and by May 23 it was at 85.4 % of its high, indicating a modest rebound that outpaced the weak velocity signal.

*Cotton.* The headline of May 2—price at a 52‑week peak while velocity was sharply negative—was confirmed by the price falling from 100 % of its high to 88.2 % by May 23, while the velocity moved from –0.11433/16 days to +0.042/16 days. That divergence was the most pronounced of the month.

In sum, the strongest confirmations were for soybeans and corn: price moved in the direction signaled by NDVI velocity. Cotton’s divergence was the most dramatic, and wheat’s mixed signals produced a lagged price reaction. Sugar’s weak velocity failed to predict a price rebound.

**THE MACRO PICTURE**

May’s NDVI signals fit into a broader narrative of near‑peak valuations and a tightening La Niña regime. The monthly aggregates show that soybeans’ velocity peaked in May and has since declined, indicating crop maturity. Corn’s velocity stayed positive into July, underscoring healthy greening and adequate moisture (LSWI above 0.4). Wheat’s velocity peaked early and has since plateaued, while sugar’s velocity peaked in July and is now declining.

The WASDE 2026/06 forecast projected U.S. soybean production at 2.65 billion bushels, a slight decline from the 2.68 billion in 2025, while corn production was forecast at 1.73 billion bushels, up from 1.68 billion. The basis for soybeans remained tight, at $0.92 per bushel, reflecting the near‑peak price and the satellite‑derived signal of a maturing crop. Corn bases were similarly tight, at $0.65, while wheat bases were slightly higher at $0.80, reflecting the lag between wheat’s positive velocity and the price rebound.

Supply‑side fundamentals—late‑season rains in the Midwest, the continued La Niña drying trend in the Gulf of Mexico, and the expectation of a mild winter in the southern U.S.—all fed into the market’s near‑peak valuations. Demand‑side pressures from China’s ongoing recovery and the EU’s green shift continued to support prices, but the satellite signals warned of potential corrections.

**SPECTRAL ALPHA**

The month’s sharpest satellite‑to‑price divergence was the Mato Grosso soybean node. On May 8, the newsletter highlighted a negative NDVI velocity of –0.16108/16 days, a clear post‑harvest decline, yet the ZS=F contract was trading at 99.7 % of its 52‑week high. The z‑score of –2.53 in the newsletter underscored the severity of the mispricing. This divergence persisted through May 23, when the velocity remained negative at –0.079/16 days, but the price had only recovered to $1,197.25, still 98.5 % of its high. The Mato Grosso node’s velocity lagged the market, suggesting that the price was still anchored to pre‑harvest optimism.

Cotton’s divergence was also noteworthy. The May 2 issue reported a velocity of –0.11433/16 days (z‑score –1.4) while the price was at a 52‑week peak. By May 23, the velocity had turned modestly positive, +0.042/16 days, but the price had fallen to 88.2 % of its high. That lag between signal and price underscores the need for caution when interpreting early-season satellite data.

**THE OUTLOOK**

1. **Soybeans in Mato Grosso** – The negative velocity is still in play. Watch for a potential price correction as the node moves into harvest. The satellite data suggest the crop is maturing faster than the market expects.

2. **Cotton in Xinjiang** – The velocity has turned positive, but the price remains below its 52‑week peak. A continued rise in greenness could support a rebound, but the market will likely remain cautious until the harvest cycle completes.

3. **Corn in Iowa** – The velocity is still negative, but the price has begun to recover. The next key phenological window is the R‑stage; a late‑season rain could shift the velocity back into the positive range and support further price gains.

4. **Wheat in Kansas** – The velocity is positive but plateauing. The upcoming stem‑hardening period will be critical; a dry spell could reverse the velocity and pressure the price.

5. **Sugar in São Paulo** – The velocity is in decline, and the price has rebounded. Monitor the LSWI; a sustained moisture deficit could trigger a further drop in velocity and price.

In the coming month, the satellite signals will continue to provide a leading indicator of crop health, but as May demonstrated, the market often lags. Traders and producers should combine NDVI velocity with on‑the‑ground observations and macro fundamentals to navigate the near‑peak valuations that define the current season.
